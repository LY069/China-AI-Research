# v2 Pod 5 — Global Investability & Non-US Regulation

**Prepared by:** v2 Pod 5 analyst · **Data as-of:** mid-2026 (current date 2026-07-10)
**Reads with:** `research/pod6_policy_geopolitics_capital.md` (US Entity List / OISP baseline — not repeated in depth here) and `research/briefs/_conventions_v2.md`
**Role:** Pod 6 (v1) built the US-centric investability lens. This pod extends it globally: what can a European, UK, Japanese, Korean, Singaporean, Taiwanese, Indian, Gulf, or other EM-domiciled investor actually own of the China AI stack — and what mechanics (Stock Connect, QFII/RQFII, ADRs, index inclusion) govern access — that a US person cannot or can.

---

## 1. Scope

This pod does not own a stack layer. It owns the **investor-domicile dimension** of investability that v1 Pod 6 left mostly US-shaped. Three questions drive the research: (1) do non-US jurisdictions run their own inbound-FDI or outbound-investment screening regimes that touch Chinese AI/semiconductor names, separate from and sometimes stricter than the US Entity List/OISP regime; (2) what are the structural access mechanics (Stock Connect, QFII/RQFII, ADR/HFCAA, HK-vs-A-share, index inclusion) that determine whether a foreign institution can actually clear, custody, and hold a given security; and (3) practically, for the ~20 most-cited China AI-stack names, who can own what. A fourth, newer thread — Beijing's own deliberations (as of July 2026) on restricting **foreign** access to Chinese AI models and possibly to equity in homegrown AI companies — is flagged as a live, unresolved, single-sourced risk that could re-shape this whole matrix.

**Framing note:** most non-US regimes discussed here are *inbound* screens (a government vetting Chinese money coming in) rather than the outbound-portfolio-investment restrictions that are the more direct US analogy (OISP). Only a handful of jurisdictions — the US, and now the EU (in a monitoring/pre-legislative phase) — have or are building anything resembling an outbound *securities* screen. This asymmetry is itself a finding: **most of the non-US world regulates the *company* (Chinese investment into them) far more than it regulates its *own investors'* ability to buy Chinese AI equity.** That leaves market mechanics (Stock Connect, QFII, ADR listing venue) — not national-security law — as the binding constraint on non-US portfolio access in most cases.

## 2. Regulatory architecture by jurisdiction

**European Union.** No Entity List/OISP equivalent exists — the EU relies on ad hoc sanctions authorities, used against Chinese entities almost exclusively for Russia-related dual-use diversion (~16 China/HK-linked entities + ~60 further export-restricted entities in a 2026 package), not AI/chip policy [WEB-874, C]. On **inbound** FDI, the EU adopted a mandatory Foreign Investment Screening Regulation overhaul (19-May-2026, in force ~summer 2026 + 18-month implementation) covering semiconductors, quantum and "certain AI technologies," including flows routed through EU entities ultimately controlled by non-EU parties [WEB-851, B]. On **outbound** — the direction that matters for "what can a European investor hold" — the EU is only at the monitoring stage: a Commission recommendation tracks (retroactively to Jan-2021) outbound SAIQ (semiconductor/AI/quantum) flows, comprehensive report due 30-Jun-2026, explicitly evidence-gathering toward a *possible* future outbound-screening regime, not yet a rule [WEB-850, B]. **As of mid-2026, a European institutional investor faces no EU-level legal bar on holding listed Chinese AI/semiconductor equity** — the constraint is market mechanics, not sanctions law. Chinese greenfield FDI into the EU has meanwhile fallen and shifted toward EV/battery plants in more accommodating states (e.g., Hungary) [WEB-852, C].

**United Kingdom.** The NSIA 2021 is an inbound tool, actively used against Chinese deals — most visibly the Feb-2026 forced divestment of UK chipmaker FTDI from a JAC Capital-led Chinese consortium, unwinding an already-closed 2021 deal [WEB-855, B]. March-2026 reforms carve out a standalone semiconductor sector while *narrowing* the AI schedule [WEB-854, B]. **The UK has no outbound investment-screening regime** and has left open (undecided) whether to build one — a UK fund faces no NSIA-style legal constraint on buying Chinese AI-stack equity today [WEB-856, C].

**Japan.** The most significant FEFTA reform since 2020 passed 29-May-2026, adding semiconductor-equipment manufacturing and AI to core sectors and creating a "high-risk foreign investor" category (SWFs, and per legal commentary Chinese investors) subject to a **1% notification threshold** vs. 50% standard — an inbound rule [WEB-857, WEB-858, B]. The more consequential outbound datapoint: Japan's ¥277tn ($1.87tn) GPIF changed its foreign-equity benchmark to **exclude mainland China A-shares** for its 2025-2030 policy period — a voluntary/prudential decision, not a legal mandate [WEB-875, C]. This is a checkable signal that large Japanese institutional capital is self-restricting onshore-China exposure absent any legal bar.

**South Korea.** No evidence found of outbound restrictions on Korean investors' access to Chinese AI/tech equity; 2026 policy energy is directed at a $500bn+ domestic chip/AI program (Samsung/SK Hynix) rather than restricting outbound China exposure. Treat Korean institutional access as unrestricted by domestic law, gated only by ordinary Connect/QFII mechanics (D — absence-of-evidence, not confirmed absence-of-regime).

**Singapore.** No inbound or outbound restriction found. Temasek raised China exposure by ~S$10bn (~$7.7bn) over the past year — its largest one-year rise since 2021 — repositioning toward AI hardware/infrastructure and robotics, while separately co-anchoring (with GIC) a $65bn Anthropic round, pursuing both US and Chinese AI exposure simultaneously [WEB-863, B]. Singapore reads as the most permissive major Asian financial center for Chinese AI-stack exposure.

**Taiwan.** The most restrictive non-US regime here, and structurally different: for capital identified as PRC-controlled (>30% direct/indirect, including disguised third-country routing), Taiwan applies a **Positive List** (permitted only in explicitly approved sectors) rather than the Negative List governing other foreign investors; CCP/military-linked entities are barred outright [WEB-861, C]. That governs mainland money entering Taiwan, but Taiwanese firms are separately reported (Jul-2026) to face tighter scrutiny of their *own* investments into mainland China tech — constraining Taiwanese capital's ability to invest into the mainland AI/semiconductor buildout, the reverse direction from most of this memo but directly relevant since Taiwan is a natural capital/expertise source for the sector [WEB-862, C].

**India.** Press Note 3 (Apr-2020) requires approval for all FDI from land-border countries (China being the operative target). A Mar-2026 Cabinet easing lets non-controlling stakes ≤10% by China-linked capital via non-land-border vehicles use the automatic route, but the government explicitly clarified this is **not** a relaxation for direct China-domiciled investors [WEB-859, WEB-860, B]. No Indian outbound restriction specific to Chinese AI-stack equity was found; access is governed by the general LRS capital-account limits applying to all foreign equity (D — inferred, not confirmed against a China-specific carve-out).

**Australia.** FIRB is an active enforcer in the critical-minerals adjacency to AI/chips: a Jan-2026 Federal Court ruling upheld an A$14m penalty for defying a forced-divestment order on rare-earths developer Northern Minerals, and 19-May-2026 reforms made critical-minerals investment mandatory-notification [WEB-871, B]. Inbound-only; no outbound restriction on Chinese AI equity found.

**Gulf/Middle East (EM).** No jurisdiction-level restriction found. Gulf SWFs (PIF, Mubadala/MGX, QIA) deployed ~$53.9bn across 108 AI deals in H1-2026, overwhelmingly into US labs/infrastructure; we could not verify Gulf-fund participation as cornerstones in the 2025-2026 China GPU-IPO wave — Biren's verified cornerstone list (Ping An, 3W Fund, Qiming, UBS, Lion Global, Eastspring) shows no Gulf name [WEB-864; D on the negative finding].

**United States (baseline contrast — see Pod 6 for detail).** OISP restricts US-person "covered transactions" into Chinese AI/semiconductor/quantum entities, but contains a **passive-investment exemption** for holdings via publicly-traded securities/index/mutual funds below defined ownership/control thresholds without MNPI or board rights [Pod 6 WEB-635/636, A]. So "US persons excluded," as stated categorically in the v1 Pod-6 table, is directionally right but **overstated for passive index exposure** — a refinement, not a reversal, of the v1 finding.

## 3. Access mechanics — how ownership actually clears

- **Stock Connect (Northbound).** Gives foreign investors of any domicile (via HK brokers) access to eligible SSE/SZSE A-shares, but **STAR Market and ChiNext stocks are restricted to institutional professional investors** even within Connect — a barrier applying regardless of nationality [WEB-865, B]. Newly-listed HK names are **not automatically Connect-eligible** — inclusion needs a market-cap/liquidity seasoning period and index-membership triggers (Hang Seng Index requires ≥24 months of listing history absent exception) — why most 2025-2026 HK AI IPOs (Zhipu, MiniMax, Biren, Iluvatar) were not Connect-eligible in their first 6-12 months, per Pod 6, corroborated here [WEB-866, C].
- **QFII/RQFII.** The unified QFII/RQFII license (2020 merger, further eased rules effective Aug-2024, old $500m/3-yr AUM bar removed) is the direct-license route into onshore A-shares [WEB-867, B] — open to institutions of any domicile (EU, Japan, Korea, Singapore, Taiwan subject to its own outbound curbs, India, Gulf), not US-specific, and not blocked for non-US persons by any foreign regime identified.
- **ADR/HFCAA.** SEC can prohibit trading if the PCAOB cannot inspect a China/HK-based auditor for two consecutive years; PCAOB regained access Dec-2022, delisting risk dormant but disclosed in every China ADR's 20-F [WEB-868, A]. This is a **US-specific** channel risk — irrelevant to HK- or A-share-only lines (Cambricon, Moore Threads, MetaX carry no US ADR) and to non-US investors holding the HK/A-share line.
- **HK vs. A-share.** A-shares carry a **30% aggregate foreign-ownership limit (FOL)** and MSCI applies an **inclusion factor** (raised from an initial 5% to 20% of free float), so even "foreign-investable" A-shares are under-represented in benchmark indices [WEB-869, B]. H-shares carry no FOL and are the more liquid line where dual-listed (Montage's H-line now trades at a premium to A — Pod 6's flagged reversal).
- **Index-inclusion effects.** EO 13959 (2020, amended 2021) forced MSCI/FTSE/S&P to strip "Communist Chinese Military Company"-designated names from global indices in Jan-2021 — a US-triggered but globally-propagated effect, since non-US investors benchmarked to those index families were structurally divested regardless of domicile [WEB-870, B]. No CMIC overlap with the current AI-chip IPO names was independently verified (unverified, D).
- **Emerging wildcard.** Reuters (7-Jul-2026, single-sourced) reports Beijing discussed with Alibaba, ByteDance and Z.ai restricting **overseas access to advanced Chinese AI models** and, separately, **limits on which investors may back homegrown AI companies** — a prospective China-side mirror of OISP. Sources say nothing is decided, scope may be prospective-only, no timeline exists [WEB-873, D]. If enacted, this would be the single biggest change to this memo's matrix.

## 5. Bottlenecks & dependencies

1. **Mechanics, not law, are the binding constraint for most non-US capital.** Absent a legal bar, the practical gating factors are Stock Connect seasoning periods for new HK listings, the STAR/ChiNext professional-investor-only rule, and QFII licensing lead times — identical friction regardless of investor domicile.
2. **Taiwan is the one non-US jurisdiction with a real, symmetric legal barrier**, restricting its own capital's ability to flow into the mainland AI/chip complex — relevant because Taiwan is otherwise a natural capital and expertise source for this sector.
3. **Japan's GPIF exclusion is a bellwether, not a rule** — if other G7-aligned pension/sovereign funds follow GPIF's prudential onshore-China exclusion, passive index-driven demand for A-share AI names could soften even without any new law.
4. **The EU's outbound-screening apparatus is embryonic** (monitoring-only through mid-2026) but structurally modeled on the US OISP precedent; a 2027+ EU outbound regime is plausible and would be the most consequential non-US regulatory event this pod could flag going forward.
5. **China's own contemplated foreign-access curbs (§3, single-sourced, D-confidence) are the largest tail risk to this entire matrix** — if Beijing restricts which investors may back homegrown AI companies, today's "Y" cells for EU/UK/Japan/Singapore/Gulf capital could convert to "C" or "N" from the Chinese side, independent of any Western regulatory action.
6. **Index-provider policy is a parallel, non-legal channel** (EO 13959/CMIC precedent) — global benchmarked capital of any domicile can be structurally excluded from names via index-committee decisions that follow US national-security triggers, even where the investor's home jurisdiction imposes no restriction of its own.

## 7. Investability read

The key refinement this pod makes to the v1 (Pod 6) picture: **the US is the outlier, not the norm.** US-domiciled capital is genuinely and increasingly excluded from the highest-momentum, Entity-List-flagged names (the GPU "four little dragons," CXMT/YMTC) — but that exclusion is a US-specific legal architecture (Entity List + OISP), not a global one. A European, UK, Japanese, Korean, Singaporean, or Gulf institution today faces **no equivalent legal bar** on most of these names — the frictions they face (Connect seasoning, STAR/ChiNext gates, QFII licensing, the 30% A-share FOL) are market-structure mechanics common to all foreign capital, not domicile-specific prohibitions. The one partial exception is Taiwan, which restricts its own capital's *outbound* flow into mainland tech by law — running the opposite direction from a US-frame reader's expectation (Taiwan limiting itself, not foreign ownership of Taiwan assets). Japan's GPIF exclusion of onshore China A-shares is a checkable signal that large G7-aligned institutional capital is voluntarily de-risking absent any legal trigger — worth watching as a template for other sovereign/pension funds. The largest unresolved risk here is not Western regulation at all: Beijing's own contemplated (Jul-2026, single-sourced) restriction on which foreign investors may hold stakes in homegrown AI companies would be the first genuinely China-side capital-account wall around this sector, converting today's broad non-US "Y" access into something narrower regardless of what any Western government does.

## GLOSSARY

- **FDI screening** — a government's legal process for reviewing (and potentially blocking or unwinding) foreign direct investment into domestic companies on national-security grounds.
- **NSI Act (NSIA)** — the UK's National Security and Investment Act 2021; the UK's inbound-FDI-screening law.
- **FEFTA** — Japan's Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act; Japan's inbound-FDI-screening law, amended May 2026.
- **HFCAA** — Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act; US law letting the SEC delist foreign issuers whose auditors the PCAOB cannot inspect for two consecutive years.
- **OISP** — Outbound Investment Security Program; US Treasury regime restricting/requiring notification for US-person investment into Chinese AI/semiconductor/quantum entities.
- **Stock Connect (Northbound/Southbound)** — the mutual-market link between the Hong Kong exchange and the Shanghai/Shenzhen exchanges; Northbound = foreign/HK money buying mainland A-shares, Southbound = mainland money buying HK stocks.
- **QFII / RQFII** — Qualified (RMB) Foreign Institutional Investor; the direct-license route for foreign institutions to trade onshore China securities outside Stock Connect.
- **ADR** — American Depositary Receipt; a US-exchange-traded instrument representing shares of a foreign (here, Chinese) company.
- **Foreign ownership limit (FOL)** — the aggregate cap (30% for China A-shares) on the share of a listed company's stock foreign investors may collectively hold.
- **Inclusion factor** — the fraction of a security's free-float market cap that an index provider (e.g., MSCI) actually counts toward index weight, used to phase in partially-restricted markets like China A-shares.
- **Positive List (Taiwan)** — Taiwan's approach to PRC-origin capital: permitted only in explicitly enumerated sectors, the inverse of the default Negative List (everything permitted except enumerated exclusions) applied to other foreign investors.
- **Press Note 3** — the Indian government notification requiring prior approval for FDI from any country sharing a land border with India (in practice, targeted at China).
- **FIRB** — Australia's Foreign Investment Review Board, the inbound-FDI-screening authority.
- **EO 13959 / NS-CMIC list** — a 2020 US executive order (amended 2021) barring US-person investment in listed "Communist Chinese Military Company"-designated securities; forced global index providers (MSCI, FTSE, S&P) to remove those names from benchmark indices worldwide.
- **WVR** — Weighted Voting Rights; a dual-class share structure (departing from one-share-one-vote) permitted under HKEX's specialist listing chapters for pre-revenue tech/biotech issuers.

## LIMITATIONS

- **Korea, Singapore, and Gulf "no restriction found" findings are negative results** — we could not identify a dedicated legal regime, which is different from confirming none exists; treat as D-confidence absence-of-evidence, not evidence-of-absence.
- **Gulf sovereign-fund participation in the China GPU-IPO wave** could not be verified in either direction with primary sources in the time available; the negative finding (no confirmed Gulf cornerstone investor) is D-confidence and could reverse with better data (e.g., a primary listing prospectus review).
- **The OISP passive-investment-exemption boundary** (what counts as "passive" enough to escape the rule) is legally nuanced and this memo gives only the directional shape; a reader relying on this for compliance purposes should consult the Treasury final rule text directly, not this summary.
- **China's own contemplated foreign-investor AI-access curbs** are single-sourced (Reuters, 7-Jul-2026), explicitly described by the sources themselves as undecided, possibly prospective-only, and without a timeline — the single largest swing factor in this memo and the most likely section to be outdated by the time this report is read.
- **Exact MSCI/FTSE weight and Stock Connect eligibility-date data for specific 2025-2026 IPOs** (Zhipu, MiniMax, Biren precise Connect-inclusion dates) were not independently re-verified against the live HKEX eligible-securities list in this cycle; Pod 6's "6-12 months" seasoning estimate is carried forward as C-confidence, not re-derived from a primary schedule.
- **Taiwan's investor-domicile matrix cells are inferred (D-confidence)** from the general PRC-capital regime and a single Digitimes report on tightened outbound scrutiny (Jul-2026); we did not find a definitive rule stating Taiwanese individuals/funds are legally barred from buying, e.g., HKEX-listed Zhipu or Biren shares through ordinary brokerage channels — the "C" grading reflects heightened-scrutiny risk, not a confirmed prohibition.
- **India LRS-vs-China-specific carve-out**: we inferred Indian investors are not specifically barred from Chinese equities beyond general capital-account limits, but did not find a primary RBI/SEBI source explicitly confirming no China-specific outbound carve-out exists — flagged D.
